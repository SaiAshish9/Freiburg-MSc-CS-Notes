# Nonlinear & Polynomial Regression

## Example: Nonlinear Regression

- Given a labeled dataset of $N$ examples $\langle (x_i, y_i) \rangle_{i=1}^N$
- Labels are generated by the **target function** $\sin(2\pi x)$ plus Gaussian noise
- **Green curve** = true function (unknown in practice)
- **Blue dots** = noisy observations
- **Goal:** recover the underlying pattern from the noisy data alone
- A straight line can't fit this, so the problem is **nonlinear**

### Data-generating process

$$
y_i = \sin(2\pi x_i) + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
$$

## Gaussian Noise

**Gaussian (normal) noise** is random error added to each label. It is drawn independently from a bell-curve distribution.

$$
\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2), \qquad
p(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mean $= 0$ | Noise is unbiased: errors are equally likely to push a label up or down |
| Variance $\sigma^2$ | Controls the noise level. Larger $\sigma$ means dots scatter further from the green curve |
| Independent | Each point's noise does not depend on the others |

**Why it matters here**
- It is why the blue dots scatter around the green curve instead of lying on it
- The model sees $y_i$, never the clean $\sin(2\pi x_i)$
- A model that fits every dot exactly is **fitting the noise**, which is **overfitting**

> [!tip] Link to squared error
> Assume $y = f(x,\theta) + \varepsilon$ with Gaussian $\varepsilon$. Then **maximum likelihood estimation** of $\theta$ is equivalent to **minimizing the sum of squared errors**.
>
> $$-\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid \mathbf{x}, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i \big(f(x_i,\theta)-y_i\big)^2 + \text{const}$$
>
> This is why squared loss is the natural choice for Gaussian noise.

## Polynomial Regression

For $x \in \mathbb{R}^1$, the polynomial model of degree $M$ is:

$$
\hat{y} = f(x, \theta) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x + \theta_2 x^2 + \dots + \theta_M x^M = \sum_{j=0}^{M} \theta_j x^j
$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $x$ | input |
| $\hat{y}$ | prediction |
| $\theta_0 \dots \theta_M$ | learned parameters (weights) |
| $M$ | degree, which controls model flexibility |

> [!note] Linear in the parameters
> The model is nonlinear in $x$ but **linear in $\theta$**. Treat $1, x, x^2, \dots, x^M$ as features and use ordinary linear regression.
> Related: feature map $g(x) = (x, x^2)$ from the SVM assignment.

## Empirical Risk

The **true risk** is the expected loss over the whole data distribution. We can't compute it, because the distribution is unknown:

$$
R(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p}\big[\, \ell\big(f(x,\theta),\, y\big) \big]
$$

The **empirical risk** replaces that expectation with the average loss over the $N$ training samples:

$$
\hat{R}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f(x_i,\theta),\, y_i\big)
$$

With **squared loss** $\ell(\hat{y}, y) = (\hat{y} - y)^2$, this gives the sum of squared errors from the slide, up to the constant factor $\frac{1}{N}$ that doesn't change the minimizer:

$$
\theta^* := \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{N} \big( f(x_i, \theta) - y_i \big)^2
$$

**Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM):** pick the $\theta$ with the lowest training loss.

- Closed-form solution: normal equations $\theta^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}$
- Alternative: gradient descent

> [!warning] ERM pitfall
> Low empirical risk does not guarantee low true risk.
> - Training error keeps falling as $M$ grows
> - The model can drive $\hat{R}$ to about 0 by memorizing the noise
> - True risk (error on new data) then goes **up**

| | Empirical risk $\hat{R}$ | True risk $R$ |
|---|---|---|
| Computed on | training data | all possible data |
| Computable? | yes | no (the distribution is unknown) |
| Estimated by | sum or mean of training losses | held-out test error |

## Choosing the Degree $M$

| Degree $M$                        | Training error | Result                                                                  |
| --------------------------------- | -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Too low (0 or 1)                  | high           | **Underfitting**: too stiff to follow the sine wave                     |
| Around 3                          | low            | **Good fit**, close to the true curve                                   |
| Too high (about 9 with 10 points) | about 0        | **Overfitting**: hits every noisy point, oscillates wildly between them |

> [!warning] Key problem
> Minimizing empirical risk alone always favors a larger $M$, since more flexibility means lower training error.

## Next: Regularization

Add a penalty on the size of $\theta$ so the model doesn't fit the noise:

$$
\theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \; \underbrace{\sum_i \big(f(x_i,\theta)-y_i\big)^2}_{\text{empirical risk}} + \lambda \|\theta\|^2
$$

# Model Selection: How to Choose Polynomial Degree $M$?

## Overview
When fitting a polynomial of degree $M$:
$$y(x, \mathbf{w}) = \sum_{j=0}^{M} w_j x^j$$
choosing the model complexity $M$ governs the **Bias–Variance Trade-off** between underfitting and overfitting.

## Comparison of Polynomial Orders

| Order ($M$) | Model Type | Regime | Behavior & Generalization |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$M = 0$** | Constant ($w_0$) | **Underfitting** | Fits a flat horizontal line at the sample mean; high bias, misses underlying structure. |
| **$M = 1$** | Linear ($w_0 + w_1x$) | **Underfitting** | Fits a straight line; too inflexible to capture the sinusoidal curvature. |
| **$M = 3$** | Cubic | **Good Fit** | Captures the true sine wave smoothly while ignoring random noise; best generalization. |
| **$M = 9$** | 9th-Order | **Overfitting** | $10$ parameters for $10$ points $\implies$ exact interpolation ($E_{\text{train}} = 0$), causing extreme oscillations and high variance. |

---

## Key Takeaways
> [!IMPORTANT] Why Training Error Fails for Model Selection
> Increasing $M$ monotonically decreases training error (reaching $0$ at $M = N - 1$), which falsely favors overly complex models.

* **Choosing $M$:** Evaluate performance on a separate **validation set** (or use $k$-fold cross-validation) to find the sweet spot before test error diverges.
* **Alternative Approach:** Use a high-capacity model ($M=9$) combined with **regularization** ($\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2$) to penalize large weight magnitudes and suppress oscillations.
