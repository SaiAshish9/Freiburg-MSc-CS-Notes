# Bias–Variance Decomposition: Visual Illustration
## Overview
This slide visualizes how **Bias** and **Variance** behave across different hypothesis sets $g(x)$ when trained on three independent datasets $D_1, D_2, D_3$ sampled from the same underlying true function $F(x)$.

* **Black Curve ($F(x)$):** The true underlying data-generating function (identical across all plots).
* **Black Dots ($D_1, D_2, D_3$):** Different finite training datasets sampled with noise from $F(x)$.
* **Red Curve ($g(x)$):** The hypothesis/model produced for each dataset.

---

## Core Concepts Illustrated
* **Bias:** How far the *average* prediction $\mathbb{E}_D[g(x; D)]$ across all possible datasets is from the true function $F(x)$.
* **Variance:** How much the learned model $g(x; D)$ fluctuates when trained on different datasets ($D_1$ vs. $D_2$ vs. $D_3$).

---

## Column-by-Column Breakdown

| Column | Model Definition $g(x)$ | Bias | Variance | Behavior Across $D_1, D_2, D_3$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **a)** | $g(x) = \text{fixed}$ (high offset) | **Very High** | **Zero** | The model ignores the training data completely ($g(x)$ is identical in $D_1, D_2, D_3$), so variance is $0$. However, the line sits far above $F(x)$, causing massive systematic error (bias). |
| **b)** | $g(x) = \text{fixed}$ (centered) | **Moderate** | **Zero** | Still completely ignores the data ($0$ variance across $D_1, D_2, D_3$), but the fixed line passes closer to the average level of $F(x)$, reducing bias compared to (a). |
| **c)** | $g(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3$ *(learned)* | **Low** | **High** | A flexible cubic polynomial can closely follow the curvature of $F(x)$ (low bias). However, because it adapts to the noise in each specific dataset, the curve shape shifts noticeably across $D_1, D_2,$ and $D_3$ (higher variance). |
| **d)** | $g(x) = a_0 + a_1 x$ *(learned)* | **Moderate–High** | **Moderate** | A learned linear model cannot capture the cubic bends of $F(x)$ (higher bias than (c)), but its slope and intercept vary less across $D_1, D_2, D_3$ than the cubic model (lower variance than (c)). |

---

## Key Takeaways

> [!IMPORTANT] The Bias–Variance Trade-off
> * **Fixed models (a, b):** Have **zero variance** because they do not depend on the training data $D$, but suffer from **high bias** because they cannot adapt to $F(x)$.
> * **Learned models (c, d):** Increasing model capacity from linear (d) to cubic (c) **decreases bias** (fits $F(x)$ better on average) at the cost of **increasing variance** (greater sensitivity to the specific training sample $D_i$).
