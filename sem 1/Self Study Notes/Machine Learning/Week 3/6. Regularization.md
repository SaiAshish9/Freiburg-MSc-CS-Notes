
**Regularization** is a technique used in machine learning to prevent **overfitting** by adding a penalty to the model's loss function when it becomes too complex.

The main objective is to make a model generalize better to unseen data rather than simply memorizing the training data.

## **1. Why do we need regularization?**

Consider a model trained on a dataset.
- **Underfitting:** Model is too simple to capture patterns in the data.
- **Good fit:** Model learns meaningful patterns and generalizes well.
- **Overfitting:** Model memorizes training data, including noise, and performs poorly on unseen data.

# Bias-Variance Tradeoff

> **Core Objective:** Find an optimal model complexity that achieves **both Low Bias** (able to fit the true underlying signal well) and **Low Variance** (able to generalize to unseen data).

---

## 1. Error Decomposition

For a target function $y = f(x) + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, the expected prediction error of a learned model $\hat{f}(x)$ at a point $x$ decomposes into three components:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \text{Bias}\left[\hat{f}(x)\right]^2 + \text{Var}\left(\hat{f}(x)\right) + \sigma^2$$

Where:
* **$\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)] = \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)$:** Error introduced by approximating a complex real-world problem with a simplified model.
* **$\text{Var}(\hat{f}(x)) = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)]\right)^2\right]$:** Sensitivity of the model to small fluctuations in the training dataset.
* **$\sigma^2$ (Irreducible Error / Noise):** Intrinsic randomness in the data generation process that **cannot be controlled** or reduced by any model.

---

## 2. Complexity Dynamics

Adjusting model capacity (e.g., polynomial degree, tree depth, number of parameters) creates a direct tradeoff:

* **Low Complexity (Left of Optimum):**
  * $\uparrow$ **High Bias** / $\downarrow$ **Low Variance**
  * **Regime:** **Underfitting** — model makes rigid assumptions and fails to capture underlying patterns.
* **High Complexity (Right of Optimum):**
  * $\downarrow$ **Low Bias** / $\uparrow$ **High Variance**
  * **Regime:** **Overfitting** — model memorizes training noise rather than learning the true signal.
* **Total Error Curve:** Follows a U-shape ($\text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} + \text{Noise}$), reaching its minimum at the optimal complexity threshold.

---

## 3. Diagnostic Summary

| Term         | High Value                        | Low Value                        |
| :----------- | :-------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| **Variance** | Risk of **overfitting**  | Good **generalization** |
| **Bias**     | Risk of **underfitting** | Good **fitting**        |
| **Noise**    | **Challenging task**     | **Easy task**           |

# Weight Decay ($L_1$ / $L_2$ Regularization)

> [!abstract] Core Concept
> **Regularization** (often called **Weight Decay** in neural networks) prevents [[Overfitting]] by adding a penalty term to the loss function. This discourages the model from learning overly complex patterns with extreme parameter values.

---

## 1. The Regularized Objective Function

Instead of minimizing only the [[Empirical Risk]] (training error), we minimize the sum of the empirical risk and a penalty term $\Omega(\theta)$:

$$\arg\min_{\theta} \left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}(y_i, f(x_i; \theta)) \right] + \alpha \Omega(\theta)$$

* **$\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal{L}(y_i, f(x_i; \theta))$:** The standard empirical risk (average loss over $N$ training samples).
* **$\Omega(\theta)$:** The regularization penalty function that measures the "size" or magnitude of the parameters $\theta$.
* **$\alpha \in \mathbb{R}_+$:** The **regularization hyperparameter** (sometimes denoted as $\lambda$). It controls the trade-off between fitting the training data well and keeping the weights small.

---

## 2. $L_1$ vs. $L_2$ Penalty Terms

The two most common ways to define the penalty $\Omega(\theta)$ across $|\theta|$ total parameters are:

| Metric | Formula | Also Known As | Key Behavior |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$L_1$ Regularization** | $\Omega(\theta) = \frac{1}{\|\theta\|} \sum_{k=1}^{\|\theta\|} \|\theta_k\|$ | [[Lasso Regression]] | **Sparsity:** Forces weak weights to become *exact zeros*, effectively performing feature selection. |
| **$L_2$ Regularization** | $\Omega(\theta) = \frac{1}{\|\theta\|} \sum_{k=1}^{\|\theta\|} \theta_k^2$ | [[Ridge Regression]] / Weight Decay | **Smoothness:** Shrinks all weights proportionally toward zero, preventing any single feature from dominating. |

> [!note] Scaling Factor
> Notice the $\frac{1}{|\theta|}$ term in these lecture slides—this simply averages the penalty over the total number of parameters so the scale of $\Omega(\theta)$ doesn't explode as the model gets larger.

---

## 3. Geometric Intuition ($L_1$ Diamond vs. $L_2$ Circle)

Visualizing optimization in a 2D parameter space ($w_1, w_2$) explains *why* $L_1$ produces zeros while $L_2$ does not:

* **Black Ellipses:** Contour lines of the unregularized loss function $\mathcal{L}$. The center is the unregularized optimum.
* **Red Diamond ($L_1$ constraint):** Defined by $|w_1| + |w_2| \le c$. Because it has **sharp corners** sitting directly on the axes, the expanding loss contours are statistically very likely to hit a corner first. At the corner shown ($\mathbf{w}_{L_1}$), $w_1 = 0$, zeroing out that feature.
* **Blue Circle ($L_2$ constraint):** Defined by $w_1^2 + w_2^2 \le c$. Because a circle is **smooth** everywhere, the loss contours hit it at a tangent point ($\mathbf{w}_{L_2}$) where both $w_1$ and $w_2$ are small, non-zero numbers.

---

## 4. Effect of $\alpha$ on Model Complexity (Polynomial Example)

Consider fitting a high-degree polynomial $f(x, \theta) = \sum_{j=0}^{15} \theta_j x^j$ to noisy sine-wave data while varying $\alpha$:

1. **Very Small $\alpha$ ($\alpha = 10^{-15}$ to $10^{-10}$):** 
   * *Result:* **[[Overfitting]] (High Variance).** The penalty is negligible, so the 15th-degree polynomial wiggles aggressively to pass through noisy data points.
2. **Optimal $\alpha$ ($\alpha = 0.0001$ to $0.001$):** 
   * *Result:* **Good Generalization.** High-order terms are suppressed, yielding a smooth curve that captures the true underlying sine wave without chasing noise.
3. **Very Large $\alpha$ ($\alpha = 5$):** 
   * *Result:* **[[Underfitting]] (High Bias).** The penalty dominates the objective function, forcing nearly all weights $\theta_j \approx 0$ and collapsing the curve into an overly flat line.
