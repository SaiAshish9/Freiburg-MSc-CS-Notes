4. Properties of Decision Trees

Decision trees are a model that makes predictions by asking a series of yes/no (or threshold) questions about the data, branching at each answer until it reaches a final outcome.

**The structure:** The root node asks the first question, such as “Is income > $50k?” Each branch is an answer, and each internal node asks a further question. Leaf nodes hold the final prediction, either a class (classification) or a number (regression).

**How they’re built:** The algorithm picks the split that best separates the data at each step. For classification, “best” usually means the largest drop in impurity, measured by Gini impurity or entropy (information gain). For regression, it’s typically the largest reduction in variance. It repeats this recursively until a stopping rule is hit, such as maximum depth or minimum samples per leaf.

**Strengths:**

- Easy to read and explain, since you can follow the path to see why a prediction was made
- Handle numeric and categorical features with little preprocessing
- Capture non-linear relationships and feature interactions

**Weaknesses:**

- Prone to overfitting if grown too deep (fixed by pruning or depth limits)
- Unstable, since small changes in the data can produce a very different tree
- Usually less accurate than ensembles

That last weakness is why trees are most often used in ensembles like random forests (many trees averaged together) and gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM), which are among the strongest methods for tabular data.

“Decision tree” also means a planning tool from decision analysis, where you map choices, chance events, and payoffs to compare options by expected value.
