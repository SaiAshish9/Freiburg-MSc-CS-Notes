# Information Gain

**Information Gain** measures how much a split on a feature reduces uncertainty (impurity) in a dataset. In decision trees, higher information gain indicates a split that separates target classes more cleanly.

---

## Notation & Setup

Suppose we evaluate a threshold split $x \le v$ on a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ of size $N$:

* **Split subsets**: $x \le v$ divides $\mathcal{D}$ into a left subset $\mathcal{D}^{(\text{L})}$ of size $N^{(\text{L})}$ and a right subset $\mathcal{D}^{(\text{R})}$ of size $N^{(\text{R})}$, where $N = N^{(\text{L})} + N^{(\text{R})}$.
* **Target distributions**:
  * $\mathrm{p}(V)$ is the distribution of the $N$ target values in $\mathcal{D}$.
  * $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{L})})$ is the distribution of the $N^{(\text{L})}$ target values in $\mathcal{D}^{(\text{L})}$.
  * $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{R})})$ is the distribution of the $N^{(\text{R})}$ target values in $\mathcal{D}^{(\text{R})}$.

---

## Mathematical Formulation

Using unnormalized entropy weighted by sample count, the **Information Gain** of the split $x \le v$ is:

$$\mathrm{I}(x \le v) = N \cdot \mathrm{H}(V) - N^{(\text{L})} \cdot \mathrm{H}\left(V^{(\text{L})}\right) - N^{(\text{R})} \cdot \mathrm{H}\left(V^{(\text{R})}\right)$$

Expanding the entropy terms $\mathrm{H}(\cdot)$ gives:

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{I}(x \le v)
&= -N \sum_{v_k \in V} \mathrm{p}(v_k) \log_2 \mathrm{p}(v_k) \\
&\quad + N^{(\text{L})} \sum_{v_k^{(\text{L})} \in V^{(\text{L})}} \mathrm{p}\left(v_k^{(\text{L})}\right) \log_2 \mathrm{p}\left(v_k^{(\text{L})}\right) \\
&\quad + N^{(\text{R})} \sum_{v_k^{(\text{R})} \in V^{(\text{R})}} \mathrm{p}\left(v_k^{(\text{R})}\right) \log_2 \mathrm{p}\left(v_k^{(\text{R})}\right)
\end{aligned}$$

> [!NOTE]
> Here, $\mathrm{H}(V) = -\sum_{v_k \in V} \mathrm{p}(v_k) \log_2 \mathrm{p}(v_k)$ is the Shannon entropy (in bits). Multiplying by $N$, $N^{(\text{L})}$, and $N^{(\text{R})}$ scales each entropy term by the number of instances in that group.

---

## Intuition & Worked Example

Consider splitting **8 emails** consisting of **4 spam** and **4 non-spam** emails:

* Target set: $V = \{\text{spam}, \text{non-spam}\}$
* Initial distribution: $\mathrm{p}(V) = \{0.5, 0.5\}$
* Initial weighted entropy: $N \cdot \mathrm{H}(V) = 8 \cdot 1 = 8$

### Split 1: Pure Separation (Maximum Gain)
* **Left**: $4 \times \text{spam}, 0 \times \text{non-spam}$ $\implies \mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{L})}) = \{1.0, 0.0\}$
* **Right**: $0 \times \text{spam}, 4 \times \text{non-spam}$ $\implies \mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{R})}) = \{0.0, 1.0\}$
* **Weighted child entropies**:
  $$N^{(\text{L})} \cdot \mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{L})}) = 4 \cdot 0 = 0, \quad N^{(\text{R})} \cdot \mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{R})}) = 4 \cdot 0 = 0$$
* **Information Gain**:
  $$\mathrm{I} = 8 - 0 - 0 = 8 \quad (\text{maximal gain in purity})$$

### Split 2: Even Mix (Zero Gain)
* **Left**: $2 \times \text{spam}, 2 \times \text{non-spam}$ $\implies \mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{L})}) = \{0.5, 0.5\}$
* **Right**: $2 \times \text{spam}, 2 \times \text{non-spam}$ $\implies \mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{R})}) = \{0.5, 0.5\}$
* **Weighted child entropies**:
  $$N^{(\text{L})} \cdot \mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{L})}) = 4 \cdot 1 = 4, \quad N^{(\text{R})} \cdot \mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{R})}) = 4 \cdot 1 = 4$$
* **Information Gain**:
  $$\mathrm{I} = 8 - 4 - 4 = 0 \quad (\text{no gain in purity})$$

---

## Summary Comparison

| Split | Left Subset | Right Subset | $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{L})})$ | $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{R})})$ | Information Gain $\mathrm{I}$ | Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Split 1** | 4 spam, 0 non-spam | 0 spam, 4 non-spam | $\{1.0, 0.0\}$ | $\{0.0, 1.0\}$ | **8** | Maximal gain in purity |
| **Split 2** | 2 spam, 2 non-spam | 2 spam, 2 non-spam | $\{0.5, 0.5\}$ | $\{0.5, 0.5\}$ | **0** | No gain in purity |

# Classification Example: Revisited (Height & Basketball Skill)

This note walks through two candidate threshold splits on a small classification dataset using **Information Gain**:

$$\mathrm{I}(x \le v) = N \cdot \mathrm{H}(V) - N^{(\text{L})} \cdot \mathrm{H}\left(V^{(\text{L})}\right) - N^{(\text{R})} \cdot \mathrm{H}\left(V^{(\text{R})}\right)$$

---

## Dataset & Initial Entropy

* **Features & Target**: Height $x$ (in cm) and basketball skill $y$.
* **Classes**: $K = 2$ target classes, $V = \{\text{g}, \text{b}\}$ (e.g., good vs. bad/beginner).
* **Dataset size**: $N = 7$ observations:
  1. $(180, \text{g})$
  2. $(170, \text{g})$
  3. $(160, \text{b})$
  4. $(170, \text{b})$
  5. $(170, \text{g})$
  6. $(160, \text{b})$
  7. $(170, \text{g})$
* **Class counts**: $4 \times \text{g}$ ($\frac{4}{7} \approx 0.57$) and $3 \times \text{b}$ ($\frac{3}{7} \approx 0.43$).
* **Initial class distribution**: $\mathrm{p}(V) = \{0.57, 0.43\}$.
* **Initial entropy**:

$$\mathrm{H}(V) = -\left(0.57 \log_2(0.57) + 0.43 \log_2(0.43)\right) \approx 0.98$$

---

## Slide 1: Split Candidate $x \le 165$

Dividing the data at height $165$ sends everyone $\le 165$ to the left region $R^{(\text{L})}$ and everyone $> 165$ to the right region $R^{(\text{R})}$:

* **Left region ($N^{(\text{L})} = 2$)**: $\{(160, \text{b}), (160, \text{b})\} \in R^{(\text{L})}$
  * Distribution: $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{L})}) = \{0.0, 1.0\}$ (purely `b`)
  * Entropy: $\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{L})}) = -\left(0 \log_2(0) + 1 \log_2(1)\right) = 0$
* **Right region ($N^{(\text{R})} = 5$)**: $\{(180, \text{g}), (170, \text{g}), (170, \text{b}), (170, \text{g}), (170, \text{g})\} \in R^{(\text{R})}$
  * Distribution: $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{R})}) = \{0.8, 0.2\}$ (4 `g`, 1 `b`)
  * Entropy: $\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{R})}) = -\left(0.8 \log_2(0.8) + 0.2 \log_2(0.2)\right) \approx 0.72$
* **Information Gain**:

$$\mathrm{I}(x \le 165) = 7 \cdot 0.98 - 2 \cdot 0 - 5 \cdot 0.72 = 3.92$$

> [!TIP]
> The split $x \le 165$ isolates the two `160` cm samples into a completely pure left child ($\mathrm{H} = 0$) while keeping most `g` samples together on the right.

---

## Slide 2: Split Candidate $x \le 175$

Dividing the data at height $175$ places all samples $\le 175$ on the left and only the tallest sample on the right:

* **Left region ($N^{(\text{L})} = 6$)**: $\{(170, \text{g}), (160, \text{b}), (170, \text{b}), (170, \text{g}), (160, \text{b}), (170, \text{g})\} \in R^{(\text{L})}$
  * Distribution: $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{L})}) = \{0.5, 0.5\}$ (3 `g`, 3 `b` — maximum impurity for 2 classes)
  * Entropy: $\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{L})}) = -\left(0.5 \log_2(0.5) + 0.5 \log_2(0.5)\right) = 1.0$
* **Right region ($N^{(\text{R})} = 1$)**: $\{(180, \text{g})\} \in R^{(\text{R})}$
  * Distribution: $\mathrm{p}(V^{(\text{R})}) = \{1.0, 0.0\}$ (pure `g`)
  * Entropy: $\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{R})}) = -\left(1.0 \log_2(1.0) + 0.0 \log_2(0.0)\right) = 0.0$
* **Information Gain**:

$$\mathrm{I}(x \le 175) = 7 \cdot 0.98 - 6 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0.0 = 0.9$$

---

## Comparison & Takeaway

| Candidate Split | Left ($R^{(\text{L})}$) | Right ($R^{(\text{R})}$) | $\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{L})})$ | $\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{R})})$ | Information Gain $\mathrm{I}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **$x \le 165$** | 2 items (`0 g, 2 b`) | 5 items (`4 g, 1 b`) | $0$ | $\approx 0.72$ | **$3.92$** |
| **$x \le 175$** | 6 items (`3 g, 3 b`) | 1 item (`1 g, 0 b`) | $1.0$ | $0.0$ | **$0.9$** |

* Between the two splits, **$x \le 165$ is much better** ($\mathrm{I} = 3.92$ vs. $\mathrm{I} = 0.9$) because $x \le 175$ leaves the large left group of 6 points evenly split (`3 g, 3 b`), resulting in maximal entropy ($\mathrm{H}(V^{(\text{L})}) = 1.0$) across most of the dataset.
