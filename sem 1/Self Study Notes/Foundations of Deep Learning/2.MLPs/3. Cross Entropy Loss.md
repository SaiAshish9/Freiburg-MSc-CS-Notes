# Convex Functions and SGD – Deep Learning

## 1. Convex Function of w

A function is called **convex** if the line segment connecting any two points on its graph lies above or on the graph.

In simple terms, a convex function has a bowl-like (U-shaped) shape.

### Mathematical Definition

A function $f(w)$ is convex if:

$$  
f(\lambda w_1+(1-\lambda)w_2)  
\leq  
\lambda f(w_1)+(1-\lambda)f(w_2)  
$$

Where:

- $w_1,w_2$ = Two points
    
- $\lambda$ = A value between 0 and 1
    
- $f(w)$ = Objective or loss function
    

### Example

Consider:

$$  
f(w)=w^2  
$$

Its first derivative is:

$$  
f'(w)=2w  
$$

Its second derivative is:

$$  
f''(w)=2  
$$

Since:

$$  
f''(w)\geq 0  
$$

The function is convex.

**Important:** For a twice-differentiable function of one variable, a non-negative second derivative everywhere guarantees convexity.

### Why Is Convexity Important in Machine Learning?

In Machine Learning, we want to minimize a loss function by finding the optimal weights $w$.

For a convex loss function:

- There are no local minima that are worse than the global minimum.
    
- Every local minimum is also a global minimum.
    
- Optimization is generally easier.
    

---

## 2. SGD (Stochastic Gradient Descent)

**SGD stands for Stochastic Gradient Descent.**

It is an optimization algorithm used to minimize a model's loss function by updating its weights.

The objective is to find weights that minimize prediction error.

### Weight Update Equation

$$  
\boxed{w_{\text{new}}=w_{\text{old}}-\eta\nabla_w L(w)}  
$$

Where:

- $w$ = Model weights
    
- $\eta$ = Learning rate
    
- $L(w)$ = Loss function
    
- $\nabla_w L(w)$ = Gradient of loss with respect to weights
    

### How Does SGD Work?

1. Initialize the model weights.
    
2. Select one training example randomly.
    
3. Calculate the prediction.
    
4. Calculate the loss.
    
5. Calculate the gradient.
    
6. Update the weights.
    
7. Repeat until the model converges or reaches a stopping condition.
    

### Example

Suppose:

$$  
L(w)=w^2  
$$

The gradient is:

$$  
\frac{dL}{dw}=2w  
$$

Assume:

- Initial weight: $w=4$
    
- Learning rate: $\eta=0.1$
    

Weight update:

$$  
w_{\text{new}}=4-0.1(2\times4)  
$$

$$  
\boxed{w_{\text{new}}=3.2}  
$$

The weight moves closer to the minimum at $w=0$.

---

## 3. Gradient Descent vs. SGD

|Gradient Descent (Batch)|SGD|
|---|---|
|Uses the entire dataset|Uses one randomly selected training example|
|One update per complete dataset|One update per training example|
|More computationally expensive per update|Less computationally expensive per update|
|Smooth convergence path|Noisy convergence path|
|Useful for smaller datasets|Often useful for large datasets|

### Mini-Batch Gradient Descent

Mini-Batch Gradient Descent updates weights using a small batch of training examples rather than the entire dataset or just one example.

It combines advantages of Batch Gradient Descent and SGD.

---

## 4. Relationship Between Convex Functions and SGD

These two concepts are connected through optimization.

- A convex loss function has a global minimum.
    
- SGD updates weights using gradients.
    
- The updates aim to move towards the minimum of the loss function.
    
- Under suitable assumptions and learning-rate conditions, SGD can converge towards the global minimum of a convex objective.
    

For non-convex functions, such as many Deep Learning neural network loss functions, finding the global minimum is not guaranteed.

---

|Concept|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Convex function|Function with a bowl-like shape|
|Weight ($w$)|Trainable parameter of a model|
|Gradient|Direction and rate of steepest increase|
|SGD|Optimization algorithm using one example per update|

### Key Takeaways

1. Convexity describes the shape of a function.
    
2. A convex function has no suboptimal local minima.
    
3. SGD stands for Stochastic Gradient Descent.
    
4. SGD updates model weights using gradients.
    
5. The learning rate controls the size of each update.
    
6. Deep Learning loss functions are generally non-convex.
    

**Remember:**

- Convexity describes the shape of the loss function.
    
- SGD describes how we update model weights to minimize the loss.

# Entropy – Deep Learning

## 1. What is Entropy?

**Entropy is a measure of uncertainty, randomness, or unpredictability in a probability distribution.**

In simple terms, entropy tells us how uncertain we are about the outcome of an event.

It is an important concept in Probability, Statistics, Machine Learning and Deep Learning.

---

## 2. Understanding Entropy with an Example

Imagine tossing a coin.

### Case 1: Fair Coin

- Probability of Heads = 0.5
- Probability of Tails = 0.5

Both outcomes are equally likely. Therefore, uncertainty is high.

### Case 2: Biased Coin

- Probability of Heads = 0.9
- Probability of Tails = 0.1

Heads is much more likely. Therefore, uncertainty is lower.

### Case 3: Certain Outcome

- Probability of Heads = 1
- Probability of Tails = 0

**Remember:** Entropy = Measure of uncertainty in a probability distribution.

## 1. What is a Bernoulli Distribution?

The **Bernoulli distribution** is a discrete probability distribution that models an experiment with exactly two possible outcomes:
- Success (1)
- Failure (0)

It is one of the simplest probability distributions and is widely used in Machine Learning, Deep Learning and Statistics.

### Example: Coin Toss

Consider tossing a coin once.

There are two possible outcomes:

| Outcome         | Value | Probability |
| --------------- | ----- | ----------- |
| Heads (Success) | 1     | $p$         |
| Tails (Failure) | 0     | $1-p$       |

For a fair coin:

$$  
p = 0.5  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
P(X=1)=0.5  
$$
$$  
P(X=0)=1-0.5=0.5  
$$

Here, $X$ is a Bernoulli random variable.

---

## 2. Probability Mass Function (PMF)

The Bernoulli distribution is mathematically represented as:

$$  
\boxed{P(X=x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}}  
$$

Where:

- $X$ = Random variable
- $x \in {0,1}$
- $p$ = Probability of success
- $1-p$ = Probability of failure

### Verification

**When X = 1:**

$$  
P(X=1)=p^1(1-p)^0=p  
$$

**When X = 0:**

$$  
P(X=0)=p^0(1-p)^1=1-p  
$$

---

## 3. Expected Value (Mean)

The expected value represents the average outcome over many repeated experiments.

$$  
\boxed{E[X]=p}  
$$

Example:

If the probability of success is 0.7:

$$  
E[X]=0.7  
$$

This means that over many trials, approximately 70% of the outcomes are expected to be successes.

---

## 4. Variance

Variance measures how much the outcomes deviate from their expected value.

$$  
\boxed{Var(X)=p(1-p)}  
$$

Example:

If $p=0.7$:

$$  
Var(X)=0.7(1-0.7)  
$$

$$  
=0.21  
$$

### Standard Deviation

$$  
\boxed{\sigma=\sqrt{p(1-p)}}  
$$
